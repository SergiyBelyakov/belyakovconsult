A hypothetical case. A donor programme helps communities restore incomes: micro-grants, vocational training, social services. Baseline survey: 37% of households had a stable source of income. Midline, a year later: 50%. The donor report gets a sentence: “the programme increased the share of households with a stable income by 13 percentage points”.

It sounds convincing. But over the same year the economy was recovering, some employers came back, pensions and benefits changed. Part of those 13 points would have happened without the programme. Here are three ways to estimate the effect and why only one of them is honest.

## 1. Before and after credits the programme with everything that changed

Comparing programme communities with themselves before the start answers “what changed”, not “what the programme changed”. Those 13 points include the general recovery, the season, other programmes and even differences in who could be surveyed.

The check is simple: look at what happened over the same time where there was no programme. In comparison communities of the same oblasts the share rose from 45% to 51%, that is by 6 points without a single grant.

## 2. An after-only comparison confuses the effect with what was there before

The second common temptation: take programme and comparison communities at the midline survey. 50% against 51%, as if the programme achieved nothing or even did harm.

But programme communities started worse off: they were chosen precisely because more people there needed help. The 8 point gap at the start did not go anywhere, and an after-only comparison simply does not see it.

## 3. Difference in differences: change minus change

It is more honest to compare changes, not levels. Programme communities rose by 13 points, comparison ones by 6. The difference of the changes, 7 points, is the estimate of the programme’s effect. On a panel of 1,800 programme households and 1,200 comparison households its 95% interval runs from +3 to +10 points: the effect is real, and it is almost half of before and after.

The method has a condition: without the programme both groups would have changed the same way. It can be checked on data from before the start. For two years before the programme the gap between the groups in registered unemployed stayed between 5.1 and 5.8 per 1,000 residents: the lines ran in parallel.

## An average effect does not mean “for everyone”

The same difference in differences by group shows whom the programme helped. For households of internally displaced people the effect is +14 points. Where the head of the household is over 60 it is +2, and where there is a member with a disability it is −3, and in both cases the interval crosses zero: the effect is not proven.

This is not a failure of the evaluation. It is an address for the programme’s next step: services that older people and people with disabilities cannot reach will not change their income.

## A check before you write “the programme increased”

1. What are you comparing with: yourselves before the start, or communities without the programme?
2. Were the comparison communities similar before the start, and did both groups move in parallel?
3. What interval does the estimate have, and does it cross zero?
4. For whom is the effect proven, and for whom not?
